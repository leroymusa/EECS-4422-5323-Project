\section{Dataset and Methods}

\subsection{Dataset description}

The dataset contains ten UV-C images of saliva deposits on five substrates (aluminum, steel, white wood, PVC, and wood) with both wet and dry patches. Each frame is accompanied by a binary mask that highlights four roughly centralized saliva patches. We treat the first eight images as the tuning set and reserve the final two images for blind testing; the masks only cover the salient regions, so every method must operate in an unsupervised fashion while relying heavily on color, contrast, and spatial locality.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Imaging:} High-contrast UV-C photography with fluorescent saliva to accentuate deposits across heterogeneous materials.
  \item \textbf{Ground truth:} One binary mask per image that marks four foreground regions, which guide plausibility filters and evaluation.
  \item \textbf{Split:} Images 1-8 serve for parameter tuning, and 9-10 are used to report unseen performance.
  \item \textbf{Challenge:} The small dataset prevents supervised training, so all methods rely on heuristics shaped by saliva brightness and location.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Method A: MATLAB thresholding pipeline}

Method A is based on MATLAB and its computer vision libraries. The idea behind this method is to create a binary mask that identifies saliva spots with high recall, using a simple and fast technique that utilizes resources already present in MATLAB. This method will convert the images to grayscale, enhance their contrast, train a model to create binary masks, and apply what it has learned to the test set.

\subsubsection{Pre-processing}

\paragraph{Luminance conversion}

The raw images, heavily red due to the UV-C setting, were imported to MATLAB and converted to luminance (black and white). The recommended ITU-R BT.601 weights to each colour channel (\(0.299 \times \text{Red}\), \(0.587 \times \text{Green}\) and \(0.114 \times \text{Blue}\)) were applied, yielding a visually correct grayscale image (Figures~\ref{fig:method_a_steps}a and~\ref{fig:method_a_steps}b).

\paragraph{CLAHE}

After this conversion, the images were treated with Contrast Limited Adaptive Histogram Equalization (CLAHE) \cite{zuiderveld1994contrast} to enhance their contrast and facilitate the identification of a suitable luminance threshold (Figure~\ref{fig:method_a_steps}c). This method was used to obtain a locally optimized histogram equalization, while avoiding noise amplification.

\subsubsection{Ground Truth evaluation}

After treating the input image, the code turns to the corresponding ground truth file. It performs an analysis of the number of white pixels the ground truth possesses and divides this quantity by the number of white areas to obtain a plausible area range to search for when segmenting the input image. This step helps filter out elements with similar luminances but that are either more than 50\% too small (noise) or too large (other image objects).

\subsubsection{Training}

\paragraph{Otsu bias tuning}

Otsu's thresholding technique \cite{otsu1979threshold} automatically classifies pixels in a luminance image as foreground (white) or background (black) depending on a threshold. Its mathematical formulation calculates the threshold that better minimizes the intra-class variance between the pixels in each class (Figure~\ref{fig:method_a_steps}d). Although it is a great method for images that generate a bimodal histogram (as those present in the dataset), it can suffer from sources with uneven lighting and end up underestimating the foreground pixels. To compensate for this effect, Method A employs a set of optimizations to produce masks that more accurately recreate the ground truth. The first one involves training a bias factor to be combined with the original threshold. This bias (Equation~\ref{eq:bias}) lowers or increases Otsu's threshold, resulting in more or fewer white pixels in the binary mask. A series of sweeps of values, ever reducing the search range, was performed to find the optimal bias.

\begin{equation}
  t_{\text{new}} = t_{\text{otsu}} \times (1 - \text{bias})
  \label{eq:bias}
\end{equation}

After this phase, a plausibility filter is used to maintain on the binary mask only the regions that have a plausible area as the saliva patches, and are roughly centralized. Their distance from the geometric center of the four ground truth saliva patches is measured, and closer regions are rewarded. After this filtering, the bias that maximizes the Dice Similarity Coefficient (DICE) between all binary masks from the training set and their respective ground truths is chosen. The DICE coefficient was chosen to combine precision and recall into a single, simple, and comparable measurement across methods.

\subsubsection{Testing}

With a defined threshold bias (Figure~\ref{fig:bias_tuning}), images 9 and 10 were processed without reference to the ground truth, only being compared to it at the end to generate the precision/recall results. Otsu's thresholding was applied, and the same two post-processing filters were adopted. The first step filtered out areas with a small pixel count to remove random noise and large areas that would not yield a feasible result for the binary mask (Figure~\ref{fig:method_a_steps}e). The second filter is similar to the centrality logic applied during training, but since the ground truth images could not be consulted, centrality was measured from the center of the image. To finalize the mask, a Close operation (Erosion + Dilation) was performed to eliminate small gaps in the generated white areas (Figure~\ref{fig:method_a_steps}f). The generated binary mask was then compared to the ground truth (Figure~\ref{fig:method_a_steps}g) for performance evaluation.

\subsection{Method B: Supervised per-channel thresholding and Linear Discriminant Analysis (LDA)}

Method B establishes a supervised baseline using the 8 tuning images and their partial ground-truth masks. The goal is to estimate pixel-level decision rules that exploit the strong fluorescence contrast of saliva under UV-C illumination. We evaluate two approaches: (i) independent per-channel thresholds and (ii) a combined linear discriminant classifier.

\subsubsection{Per-channel threshold learning}

For each channel \(c \in \{R,G,B\}\), we compute the distribution of pixel intensities for saliva and background using the labeled tuning subset. We sweep all unique intensity values as candidate thresholds and compute the Dice coefficient \cite{dice1945measures} obtained when predicting saliva whenever \(x_c \geq t\). The optimal threshold for each channel is chosen as:

\begin{equation}
  t_c = \arg\max_t \text{Dice}(t)
\end{equation}

A pixel is classified as saliva if any channel exceeds its optimal threshold:

\begin{equation}
  y_{R,G,B} = 1 \text{ if } R \geq t_R \vee G \geq t_G \vee B \geq t_B
\end{equation}

This provides a simple supervised baseline in which each channel contributes independently.

\subsubsection{Linear Discriminant Analysis (LDA)}

While independent thresholds produce axis-aligned decision boundaries, the RGB channels are strongly correlated. As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:correlation_heatmap}, saliva pixels exhibit extremely strong positive correlations across all three channels (0.97--0.99), meaning that their colour variation is almost entirely explained by a single underlying intensity dimension. In contrast, background pixels show weak or even negative cross-channel correlations, indicating substantially greater chromatic variability.

This difference motivates a multivariate classifier that can exploit joint RGB patterns rather than thresholding each channel independently.

We therefore learn a single discriminant direction \(\mathbf{w} \in \mathbb{R}^3\) using Linear Discriminant Analysis (LDA) \cite{fisher1936use}. LDA computes a projection vector

\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{w} = \mathbf{S}_w^{-1}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\text{saliva}} - \boldsymbol{\mu}_{\text{bg}})
\end{equation}

that maximizes the separation between saliva and background, where \(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\text{saliva}}\) and \(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\text{bg}}\) are the class means, and \(\mathbf{S}_w\) is the pooled within-class covariance matrix.

Each pixel is mapped to a one-dimensional score \(s = \mathbf{w}^T\mathbf{x}\).

We then sweep all unique score values on the tuning set and select the threshold \(T\) that maximizes the Dice coefficient \cite{dice1945measures}.

The resulting classifier is

\begin{equation}
  y = \begin{cases}
    1 & \text{if } \mathbf{w}^T\mathbf{x} \geq T \\
    0 & \text{otherwise}
  \end{cases}
\end{equation}

This creates an oblique decision boundary in RGB space, enabling LDA to exploit correlated variations across channels and improving robustness relative to independent per-channel thresholds.

\subsubsection{Pre- and Post-Processing}

Before classification, a Gaussian blur is applied to suppress sensor noise while preserving the low-frequency structure of saliva fluorescence.

After thresholding, morphological closing and opening remove isolated noise pixels and fill small gaps, producing more coherent saliva regions.

\subsection{Method C: exploratory k-means clustering}

Method C looks at using K-Means clustering \cite{macqueen1967some} to distinguish between saliva pixels and background pixels. K-Means clustering is an unsupervised machine learning algorithm used to partition a dataset into \(k\) distinct, non-overlapping subgroups (clusters). In the context of this image segmentation project, each pixel in the image is treated as a data point in a 3-dimensional color space (Red-Green-Blue).

The algorithm works iteratively to assign each data point to one of \(k\) groups based on feature similarity. The goal is to group pixels of similar color and intensity together. This is effective for saliva detection as the target (saliva) possesses distinct colorimetric properties (high brightness) compared to the background in each of the provided raw images.

\subsubsection{Pre-processing}

Standard image segmentation pipelines often begin with Gaussian blurring or Median filtering to reduce noise. However, for this specific dataset, no pre-processing filters were applied, as it was discovered that there was an overall improvement in Accuracy, Precision, and DICE Score results without filters (though there was a very slight decrease in Recall).

\subsubsection{K-means}

The K-means algorithm operates directly on RGB pixel values. To ensure robustness, the algorithm is executed 10 times with different random initializations, and the result with the best compactness metric (the smallest cluster variance) is selected. This multi-run approach mitigates the sensitivity of K-means to initial centroid placement, which can otherwise lead to suboptimal local minima.

After clustering, the center (centroid) of each cluster is computed. To identify which cluster corresponds to saliva, we calculate the perceived luminance for each cluster center using the standard RGB-to-luminance conversion: \(Y = 0.114 \cdot B + 0.587 \cdot G + 0.299 \cdot R\). The cluster with the brightest center (highest luminance) is designated as the saliva cluster. Finally, all pixels belonging to the saliva cluster in the original image are assigned a value of 255, while all other pixels are set to 0, creating the binary segmentation mask.

\paragraph{Mathematical Foundation}

The algorithm aims to minimize the objective function \(J\):

\begin{equation}
  J = \sum_{j=1}^{k} \sum_{i=1}^{n} \| \mathbf{x}_i^{(j)} - \mathbf{c}_j \|^2
\end{equation}

Where:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \(k\) is the number of clusters.
  \item \(\mathbf{x}_i^{(j)}\) is a pixel point belonging to cluster \(j\).
  \item \(\mathbf{c}_j\) is the centroid (geometric center) of cluster \(j\).
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Algorithm Configuration}

The implementation uses \(\texttt{cv2.kmeans}\) with the following specific tuning to ensure robustness:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Criteria:} The algorithm stops if it reaches 100 iterations or if the centers move by less than \(\epsilon = 0.2\) (convergence).
  \item \textbf{Stability (The `attempts' parameter):} The code executes the K-means algorithm \textbf{10 distinct times} using different random initializations.
    \begin{itemize}
      \item K-means is sensitive to the initial placement of centroids; a bad starting guess can lead to a ``local minimum'' (a suboptimal result).
      \item By running it 10 times (\(\texttt{attempts=10}\)) and selecting the result with the best compactness, we ensure the segmentation is mathematically stable and repeatable.
    \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Cluster Selection via Luminance}

Once the pixels are grouped into \(k\) clusters, we must determine which cluster represents ``saliva.'' We utilize a Luminance calculation based on the perception of brightness.

The code calculates the luminance (\(Y\)) for each cluster center using the standard coefficients for RGB (Red-Green-Blue) images:

\begin{equation}
  Y = 0.114 \cdot B + 0.587 \cdot G + 0.299 \cdot R
\end{equation}

We sort the calculated luminance values and select the cluster with the maximum luminance index as the positive class (Saliva). The logic behind this decision is that saliva is bright in each of the UV images, resulting in high pixel intensity.

\subsubsection{Post-Processing (Morphological Filtering)}

Raw K-means output often contains noise (isolated pixels) or gaps. We apply Morphological Transformations using a \(5 \times 5\) kernel to refine the binary mask.

\textbf{Morphological Opening:}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Operation:} Erosion followed by Dilation.
  \item \textbf{Purpose:} This removes small, isolated specs of noise (false positives) from the background while keeping the larger saliva regions intact.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Morphological Closing:}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Operation:} Dilation followed by Erosion.
  \item \textbf{Purpose:} This closes small black holes inside the white saliva regions (false negatives), ensuring the predicted saliva mass is solid and continuous.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Discussion}

Experiments were conducted with \(K=2, 3, 4, 5\) clusters. The \(K=2\) configuration performed worst in terms of precision. The \(K=3\) configuration provides a good balance between precision and recall. While \(K=4\) also offers a reasonable balance, it begins to lose recall compared to \(K=3\). The \(K=5\) configuration loses too much recall to be practical.

The performance is evaluated using four metrics. Given the medical nature of the data (imbalanced classes where the background is much larger than the saliva), \textbf{Accuracy} is often misleading.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Accuracy:} Overall correctness. Usually high (\(>90\%\)) because the vast majority of the image is background (True Negatives).
  \item \textbf{Precision:} High precision indicates we are not over-segmenting (classifying background as saliva).
  \item \textbf{Recall:} High recall means we aren't missing subtle saliva traces.
  \item \textbf{Dice Score (F1):} The harmonic mean of Precision and Recall.
    \begin{equation}
      \text{Dice} = \frac{2 \cdot TP}{2 \cdot TP + FP + FN}
    \end{equation}
    The Dice score provides the most balanced view of performance for segmentation tasks. A Dice score closer to 1.0 indicates a perfect overlap between the algorithm's prediction and the ground truth.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Method D: probabilistic Gaussian mixture model}

Method D upgrades Method C's k-means clustering with a full Gaussian mixture model (GMM) that quantifies uncertainty and chooses the most probable segmentation. Each pixel \(\mathbf{x}_i = (R_i, G_i, B_i)^\top\) is modeled as a sample from a mixture of \(K\) Gaussian distributions:

\[
  p(\mathbf{x}_i \mid \Theta) = \sum_{k=1}^{K} \pi_k \, \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x}_i \mid \boldsymbol{\mu}_k, \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_k),
\]

where \(K \in \{3,4\}\), \(\pi_k\) are mixture weights (\(\pi_k \ge 0\), \(\sum_k \pi_k = 1\)), \(\boldsymbol{\mu}_k \in \mathbb{R}^3\) are mean RGB vectors, \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_k \in \mathbb{R}^{3 \times 3}\) are full covariance matrices, and \(\Theta = \{\pi_k, \boldsymbol{\mu}_k, \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_k\}_{k=1}^K\) are all GMM parameters.

\subsubsection{Pre-processing}

The images remain in RGB space and are reshaped into pixel arrays so the GMM learns the native color distribution directly. Candidate means \(\boldsymbol{\mu}_k^{(0)}\) are initialized using Method C's k-means centers, helping the Expectation–Maximization (EM) algorithm start near plausible saliva/background separations. No additional smoothing or filtering is applied, keeping preprocessing comparable to the other pipelines.

\subsubsection{Expectation–Maximization algorithm}

The parameters \(\Theta\) are estimated via the Expectation-Maximization (EM) algorithm \cite{dempster1977maximum}, implemented by \(\texttt{sklearn.mixture.GaussianMixture}\):

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{E-step (responsibilities):}
    \[
      \gamma_{ik} = p(z_i = k \mid \mathbf{x}_i, \Theta^{(t)}) = \frac{\pi_k^{(t)} \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x}_i \mid \boldsymbol{\mu}_k^{(t)}, \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_k^{(t)})}{\sum_{j=1}^K \pi_j^{(t)} \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x}_i \mid \boldsymbol{\mu}_j^{(t)}, \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_j^{(t)})}
    \]
  \item \textbf{M-step (parameter update):}
    \begin{align}
      \pi_k^{(t+1)} &= \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N \gamma_{ik}, \\
      \boldsymbol{\mu}_k^{(t+1)} &= \frac{\sum_{i=1}^N \gamma_{ik} \mathbf{x}_i}{\sum_{i=1}^N \gamma_{ik}}, \\
      \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_k^{(t+1)} &= \frac{\sum_{i=1}^N \gamma_{ik} (\mathbf{x}_i-\boldsymbol{\mu}_k^{(t+1)})(\mathbf{x}_i-\boldsymbol{\mu}_k^{(t+1)})^\top}{\sum_{i=1}^N \gamma_{ik}}
    \end{align}
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection{Multiple initializations and model selection}

EM only guarantees convergence to a local optimum of the log-likelihood. To address this, for each image and each \(K\), we run \(n_{\text{runs}} = 10\) independent GMM fits, mixing k-means seeds with random starts to explore multiple basins of attraction.

For each run \(r\), we compute the pseudo-log-likelihood (PLL):

\[
  \text{PLL}(\Theta_r) = \sum_{i=1}^{N} \log p(\mathbf{x}_i \mid \Theta_r) = N \cdot \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N \log p(\mathbf{x}_i \mid \Theta_r),
\]

which evaluates the model fit on the same data used for training. We select the best run:

\[
  r^* = \arg\max_r \text{PLL}(\Theta_r),
\]

using \(\Theta_{r^*}\) for the rest of the pipeline. This directly implements the principle of choosing the most probable solution.

\subsubsection{From probability maps to binary masks}

For the selected model \(\Theta^*\), EM yields responsibilities \(\gamma_{ik} = p(z_i = k \mid \mathbf{x}_i, \Theta^*)\). To identify the saliva (foreground) component, we compute the luminance for each component mean \(\boldsymbol{\mu}_k = (R_k, G_k, B_k)\):

\[
  Y_k = 0.299 R_k + 0.587 G_k + 0.114 B_k,
\]

and select \(k_{\text{fg}} = \arg\max_k Y_k\) as the brightest component. The foreground probability map is then:

\[
  P_{\text{FG}}(\mathbf{x}_i) = \gamma_{ik_{\text{fg}}} = p(z_i = k_{\text{fg}} \mid \mathbf{x}_i, \Theta^*).
\]

We threshold this probability map at \(T = 0.5\) to create a binary mask:

\[
  M(i) = \begin{cases}
    255, & P_{\text{FG}}(\mathbf{x}_i) > 0.5, \\
    0,   & \text{otherwise}.
  \end{cases}
\]

Finally, we apply morphological opening and closing using a \(5 \times 5\) kernel to remove small noisy blobs and fill small holes, yielding the final cleaned saliva mask.

\subsubsection{Results}

Table~\ref{tab:method-d-results} reports the average metrics across all images for \(K=3\) and \(K=4\). The probabilistic GMM achieves high recall (\(0.897\) at \(K=3\), \(0.630\) at \(K=4\)) by capturing bright saliva pixels that hard clustering sometimes misses. At \(K=3\), the model prioritizes recall over precision (\(0.272\)), resulting in many false positives due to Gaussian overlap.
