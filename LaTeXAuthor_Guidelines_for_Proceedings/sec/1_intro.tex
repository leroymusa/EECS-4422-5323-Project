{\section{Introduction}}

\subsection{Problem Definition}

Hospital-acquired infections (HAIs) pose a recurring threat to healthcare environments, increasing patient risk, complicating workflows, and incurring higher operational costs. HAIs spread by direct contact, droplets, aerosols, and contaminated surfaces. The problem is further harmful in busy clinical areas, where equipment is moved frequently, and patients are vulnerable. Major reviews and guidelines identify environmental reservoirs, device-related contamination, and workflow pressures as the primary factors contributing to disease propagation in hospitals, suggesting that broad infection-control policies alone are insufficient. Additionally, knowing where contamination actually accumulates is crucial, allowing for focused and effective cleaning \cite{allegranzi2011hand,loveday2014epic3,otter2013evidence,weber2013understanding,magill2014multistate}.

Saliva is a common target for efforts aimed at reducing HAIs because it carries many pathogens and is easily spread during talking, coughing, and routine procedures. Once saliva lands on instruments or surfaces, its appearance changes as it dries or interacts with different materials, making it difficult to spot under normal lighting. Studies using fluorescence reveal clear differences between moist and dried saliva on various surfaces, indicating that optical imaging can detect deposits that would otherwise be missed \cite{miranda2014fluorescence,wu2024quantitative}. Other reviews and studies that treat saliva as a diagnostic and surveillance fluid also highlight its role in pathogen carriage and transmission, reinforcing the idea that detecting saliva specifically is a useful way to reduce HAIs \cite{malamud2011saliva,to2020consistent,oliveira2024emergence}.

In practice, hospitals employ a layered approach to prevention, utilizing masks, face shields, and routine cleaning and sterilization. Masks and shields reduce the spread of droplets but do not eliminate surface deposition. Experiments that use fluorescent surrogates often reveal contamination patterns invisible to the naked eye, demonstrating that barriers and cleaning must work together \cite{shah2021assessment}. Mask effectiveness depends on fit, material, and how long it is worn. Leaks and saturation can still let droplets escape and contaminate nearby surfaces \cite{chang2020aerial}. Face shields block splashes and large droplets but do not prevent peripheral deposition, and the shield surfaces themselves can become contaminated. Therefore, they are most effective when used in conjunction with masks \cite{shah2021assessment,chang2020aerial}. Routine disinfection lowers surface bioburden, but actual coverage and contact time vary in real settings. Laser-induced fluorescence (LIF) can map where droplets land and persist, providing useful feedback to improve cleaning patterns and identify missed spots \cite{wu2024quantitative}.

Computer vision can make these imaging findings actionable. Segmentation algorithms can produce binary masks that mark saliva or fluorescent surrogates in images. These masks can guide automated or semi-automated cleaning tools to focus on contaminated regions. Fluorescence imaging and high-speed visualization provide the contrast and ground truth necessary to train and validate these algorithms across various surface types and moisture states, helping bridge the gap between lab measurements and real-world cleaning workflows \cite{miranda2014fluorescence,shah2021assessment,chang2020aerial,wu2024quantitative}.

\subsection{Literature Review}

Medical image segmentation is the area of medical technology that most resembles the creation of binary masks for saliva recognition. This process labels every pixel (or voxel) in an image to isolate organs, lesions, fluids, usually by difference in contrast. For contamination detection this usually boils down to a binary mask (contaminant vs. background), but the goals are the same as other clinical tasks: get accurate boundaries, be robust to different imaging conditions, and produce outputs that can drive decisions or automated actions. Standard overlap metrics such as the Dice Coefficient and Intersection over Union (IoU) are used to judge how well a model matches expert masks, called Ground Truths (GT). These metrics guide model selection and tuning \cite{ren2022medical}.

The methods for segmentation range from image-processing techniques to deep neural networks. Basic steps, such as contrast enhancement, thresholding, edge filters (Sobel/Laplace), and morphological cleanup, are still used for boosting visibility of small or low-contrast deposits, as well as for preprocessing images before applying the model \cite{ren2022medical,deng2011edge}. On the learning side, U-Net and its many variants are the baseline because they combine multi-scale context with precise localization. More recent work adds attention blocks or mixes CNNs with transformers to capture longer-range context and improve small-object detection \cite{rahman2021exploring,dwivedi2025optimizing}. There’s also a growing push to use synthetic data and generative models to expand training sets, and public codebases for droplet tasks (e.g., Droplet-ML) make it easier to reproduce experiments and try augmentation strategies \cite{pham2024enhanced,sarritzu2024dropletml}.

The main challenges for saliva/droplet segmentation are familiar in the computer vision field. Getting enough labeled masks is expensive, droplets are tiny so class imbalance is severe, and appearance changes a lot with surface type, lighting, and drying state. That means the necessity of specialized datasets, multi-scale supervision, and careful preprocessing or domain-adaptation to generalize from lab images to real clinical scenes \cite{rahman2021exploring,dwivedi2025optimizing}. Practically speaking, the best routes adopt a hybrid approach, using classical enhancement to make deposits visible \cite{ren2022medical}, training robust segmentation networks (with attention to small-object handling) \cite{dwivedi2025optimizing}, and relying on synthetic or open datasets to fill gaps \cite{pham2024enhanced,sarritzu2024dropletml}.

\subsection{Contributions}

In this work we deliver:
\begin{itemize}
    \item A structured comparison of the existing MATLAB/thresholding pipeline (Method A), the exploratory k-means clustering pipeline (Method C), and the probabilistic Gaussian mixture model pipeline (Method D).
    \item A GMM workflow that seeds EM with k-means centers, runs ten initializations per image, selects the best solution via pseudo-log-likelihood, and thresholds the resulting probability map before morphological cleanup.
    \item Empirical evidence that the \(K=4\) GMM increases Dice while keeping recall above \(0.96\), illustrating how likelihood-aware model selection improves the unsupervised segmentation of saliva under UV-C.
\end{itemize}
